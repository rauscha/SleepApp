There is no hurry, and there is no way to arrive early, because the temple has been at the top of these stairs for six hundred years and it will still be there whenever you get to it.

You start in the low part of the valley, where the air is warm and green and smells of wet leaf. The path is easy here. It is not stairs yet. It is only a track through the trees with roots across it, worn pale on top where feet have polished them. [pause] Your pack is light. You made sure of that, days ago, in a room far from here, taking things out and putting them back and taking them out again.

The first step is not obviously the first step. That is how these old ways are. The path simply gets steeper, and then there is a flat stone set into the hill, and then another, and by the time you understand that you are climbing stairs you have already climbed nine of them. [softly] No one announced it. It just began.

The stones are grey and rounded at the edges. Not cut round — worn round. Six hundred years of rain and six hundred years of feet.

You climb slowly. There is a rhythm that works and you find it in the first few minutes: step, step, breathe. Step, step, breathe. It is slower than you would walk on flat ground, and that is correct, and after a while you stop having to think about it at all. [pause]

The forest closes over the path. It is not dark, exactly. It is green — light coming down through many layers of leaf, arriving softened and cool, the way light does through water.

Somewhere off to your left there is water moving. You cannot see it. You will not see it for another hour. But it is there, a low continuous sound below and to the side, and it will be with you most of the way up.

[softly] It is a good sound to walk to.

The stairs turn. They do not switchback sharply — they curve, the way a thing curves when it was laid down by people who were following the shape of the hill rather than arguing with it. There is no engineering here. There is only accommodation. Where there was a boulder, the stairs go around the boulder. Where there was a spring, the stairs step wide of it, and the stone there is darker and slick with a hundred generations of wet.

You step wide of it too. [pause]

Your legs have found the work. The first ten minutes were the negotiation. Now the negotiation is over and your body has agreed, and there is a warmth in the thighs that is entirely pleasant, the good kind, the kind that means the day is being used well.

Step, step, breathe.

There are small shrines at intervals. You do not know the interval — it is not regular. A niche cut into the rock at the side of the path, with a stone figure in it, worn so smooth by weather that the face is only a suggestion of a face now. Someone has left rice. Someone has left a coin, green with age. Someone has left three flowers that have gone brown but not yet fallen apart. [pause]

You do not know who left them or when. It could have been this morning. It could have been last spring.

You stop for a moment at one of them, not out of belief, only because it is a place where people stop, and it seems right to.

You think, briefly, about the stairs themselves — about the fact that every one of these stones was carried here.

Not delivered. Carried. Up this same path, by people, one at a time, before there was any path to carry them up. Someone was first. Someone stood at the bottom of an ordinary wooded hill with a flat stone and an idea about where it should go, and then did that again, and then a great many people did it again for a very long time. [pause]

It took generations. Nobody who started it saw it finished. That was understood at the time and it did not stop anyone.

[softly] You are walking up something that was built by people who knew they would not see the top of it.

The thought is not heavy. It is oddly restful — the same restfulness as a very old tree, or a river that was here first. You are not required to finish anything either.

The forest breathes around you. That is the only word for what it does — a slow movement of air up the valley, arriving, going, arriving again, moving the high leaves and not the low ones. [softly] The low ones stay entirely still.

You go on.

The stairs get steeper for a while. Not hard, only steeper, and you shorten the rhythm without deciding to: step, breathe. Step, breathe. Your hand finds the rock face on the inside of the curve and rests there lightly as you climb, and the rock is cool and faintly damp and covered in a moss so fine it feels like felt.

[pause]

Somebody is coming down.

You hear them before you see them — a slow, even sound, the particular double-tap of a walking stick and a foot, over and over, from around the curve above. It takes a long while to arrive. When it does it is an old woman in a grey jacket with a basket on her back, coming down the stairs sideways, one step at a time, the way people do when their knees have opinions.

She sees you. You stand aside on the wide part of the step. [softly] She says something you do not understand, in a tone you understand completely — something like there you are, or nearly there, or the weather is holding.

You say something back in your own language, in the same tone.

She laughs once, briefly, and goes on down. The double-tap gets quieter behind you and then the curve takes it and then there is nothing.

[pause]

You stand there a moment longer than you need to.

That was the whole exchange. Nobody's day changed. And yet you go on up feeling better than you did, for no reason that would survive being written down.

There is a bird here that makes two notes, the second lower than the first. It makes them, and waits a long time, and makes them again. It has been doing this since you started. You noticed it about a hundred steps ago and now you cannot stop hearing it, and rather than being an irritation it has become a kind of clock — the slowest clock, marking out a time that has nothing to do with minutes.

Two notes. A long wait. Two notes.

The water sound is closer now, on the left, below.

You come around a curve and there is a gap in the trees and you can see out, and down, and back — the whole valley you climbed out of, laid out green and hazy, the roofs of the village at the bottom so small now that they are just a texture. [softly] You came from there. It took a long time and it did not feel like a long time.

You stand and look for a while. There is no reason to hurry the looking either.

The air up here is different. Cooler. Thinner in a way you feel in the top of your chest rather than anywhere else. It smells less like leaf now and more like stone and cold water.

[pause]

You go on.

The stairs here are narrower and the trees are shorter — pine now, not broadleaf, and their needles have made a soft brown carpet in the corners of every step, drifted there by years of wind. Your boots make almost no sound. That is the thing you notice most. You are climbing a mountain and you are making almost no sound at all.

The two-note bird has been left behind, or has stopped. You cannot tell which.

Step, breathe. Step, breathe.

Somewhere in here your thoughts get quieter. It is not that they stop. It is that they get further apart, the way the shrines do, and the space between them gets filled with the sound of your own breathing and the sound of the water below and the very small sound of your boots in the pine needles. [softly] There is nothing to solve up here. Nothing you brought up with you is going to be solved on these stairs.

So you carry it a while longer and it gets lighter anyway.

The stone under your hand changes color. It has been grey the whole way and now there is a band of something warmer in it, ochre, running diagonally through the rock face, and you follow it with your eyes as you climb until it goes up out of sight above you.

[pause]

There is a bench. Not a built bench — a flat stone at the side of a landing, set there deliberately, worn into a shallow dish in the middle by everyone who has ever sat on it.

You sit on it.

Your pack comes off and the cool goes straight through the back of your shirt where the pack was, and it is one of the best feelings there is, and it lasts about a minute. You let it last the whole minute.

The valley is below. The mist is coming up it in the late part of the afternoon, the way it does — not falling, rising, a slow white filling-in from the bottom. The village is gone under it now. The lower forest is going. [softly] By dark the whole valley will be a lake of cloud and you will be above it.

You drink some water. It is cold and tastes faintly of the metal bottle and that is exactly right.

Your boots are dusty to the ankle and there is a pale line across each one where the pine needles have polished the leather. You look at them for a while without thinking anything about them. [softly] They have done well.

There is a pattern in the stone of this landing — a spiral, or most of a spiral, cut shallow and nearly worn away. Somebody's work. Somebody's afternoon, a long time ago, on a step where people would sit.

You put your thumb in the groove of it and follow it around once. It is smooth. Everybody does this. [pause]

Above you the stairs go up into shadow and there are perhaps two hundred left. You do not count them, but you know it is roughly that, the way you know how much water is left in a bottle by the weight.

Two hundred is nothing. Two hundred is twenty minutes of step, breathe.

[pause]

The water sound has changed. It is no longer below you and to the left. It is ahead, and higher, and it has more air in it — a falls, somewhere up there, still out of sight.

You put the pack back on. The cool spot on your back disappears.

Step, breathe.

The stairs cross the water eventually. There is a bridge, if you can call it that — three long slabs of stone laid side by side across a gap, no rail, moss on the upstream edges, and beneath them the creek going down through a chute of polished rock, white and quick and very loud for something so narrow.

You stop in the middle of it, because everybody stops in the middle of it, and look upstream.

The falls are there — not tall, twenty feet, dropping into a green pool between two walls of wet rock. The air above the pool is full of the finest possible water, not rain, not mist, just a coolness with weight to it, and it settles on your face and your forearms in the time it takes to look. [pause]

There is a rainbow in it, very small, low down, in the corner where the last sun is getting in.

It will be there for another few minutes and then the sun will move off the gap and it will not be there, and tomorrow at about this time it will be back.

[softly] Nobody arranged that. It simply happens here, most afternoons, whether or not anyone is standing on the bridge.

You cross it in four steps and the sound falls away behind you almost immediately, muffled by the trees, back to being a thing below and to the side. [softly] It is strange how quickly a loud thing becomes a quiet one.

Now the last part.

You know it is the last part because the stairs change. They are cut, here — properly cut, squared, with an edge, laid by someone with a chisel and an intention. Somebody decided that the final approach should feel different underfoot, and six hundred years later it still does.

[pause]

The trees open.

The light is orange now — low, coming in flat from the west across the top of the cloud-lake in the valley, and everything it touches goes warm: the stone, the trunks, the moss, the backs of your hands.

There is a gate. Wood, grey, no door in it, standing open the way it has stood open for longer than anyone remembers. You go through it and nothing happens. There is no threshold feeling, no click. You simply go through and now you are on the other side and the stairs go on for a few more steps.

And then they stop.

[softly] They stop the way they started — without announcing it.

The courtyard is small. Smaller than you expected, and that is a relief rather than a disappointment. Flagstones, mostly level, grass between them. A wall on the valley side, low, with the cloud coming up behind it. A single tree in the corner, very old, propped up on one side with a wooden brace that is itself old.

The temple building is dark timber and it is not large. There is a bell. Nobody is ringing it.

[pause]

There is nobody here at all, in fact, or nobody you can see. There is a broom leaning against a post. There is a line of washing, three cloths, moving very slightly. There is a cat asleep on the warm stone by the wall who opens one eye and closes it again.

You put your pack down.

You sit on the step, facing the valley, and the stone is holding the whole day's heat and it comes up through you slowly.

Your legs are humming. That good, used, warm hum. You stretch them out in front of you and they are heavy and pleased and entirely finished with climbing.

[softly] There is nowhere further up.

The cloud in the valley has come nearly to the top of the low wall now. It moves the way cloud does from above — slowly, silently, with an internal turning-over. The last of the sun is on it and it is going gold and then rose and then a dull pink and then it will go grey and then it will be night.

You watch that happen. It takes a long time. Nothing about it is quick and no part of it needs you.

[pause]

Somewhere inside the building someone moves — a footstep on wood, one, and then nothing more. The bell does not ring.

The air goes cool all at once, the way mountain air does when the sun leaves it.

You do not get up to find a blanket yet. The stone under you is still giving back its heat and it will for another hour, and you have your back against a post, and your pack beside you, and the cat has resettled somewhere in the dark with a small sound.

Two notes, far below. The lower one after the higher one. The bird, or another bird, somewhere down in the part of the valley that is already under cloud. [softly] A long wait. And then again.

The first star comes out over the ridge opposite. Then, some time later, another one.

Your eyes are heavy. Your legs are heavy in the good way. The stone is warm and the air is cold and you are exactly the right amount of tired — the amount that has been earned by six hundred years of stairs and one afternoon of your own walking.

[pause]

You could go in. There is a room, and a mat, and you know that because you were told there would be.

In a while.
