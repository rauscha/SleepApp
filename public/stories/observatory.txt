The road up to the observatory is nine miles of switchbacks, and you drove it slowly, in the last of the light, with the windows down.

You are here now. The car is parked and cooling behind you, ticking softly to itself the way engines do, and the door of the dome is open, and the air coming down off the ridge smells like cold stone and juniper. [pause] There is no one else on this mountain tonight. There is no one else for nine miles.

You take your time on the steps. There are eleven of them, poured concrete, worn smooth in the middle where eighty years of people have put their feet in the same places. [softly] You put your feet in the same places.

Inside, the dome is dark and enormous and it holds the day's warmth the way a barn does. You do not turn on the lights. There is a red lamp by the desk, and you switch that on instead, and the whole room goes the color of a held breath. Red light does not spoil your eyes. Red light lets the dark stay in you. [pause]

The telescope is above you in the dark, a long shape you know better by feel than by sight, and it is already pointed where you left it.

You sit down in the chair. It is an old wooden chair with arms polished pale by forearms, and it accepts your weight with one small sound and then nothing.

There is nothing to do yet. The sky is not dark enough. [pause] This is the part of the night you like best, and you have never told anyone that, because it would be difficult to explain that the best part of looking at stars is the half hour before you can.

So you sit. [softly] So you sit, and you let the room get darker around you without helping it.

Through the slot in the dome there is a stripe of sky, and it is that particular blue that has no name — not the blue of afternoon, not black yet, the blue that comes after the sun is gone but before the sky admits it. It deepens while you watch. It does not seem to move at all, and then you look away, and look back, and it has moved.

The first star is not a star. You know this. It is Venus, low in the west, and it arrives the way it always arrives, which is to say you cannot find the moment it appeared. It simply becomes true that it is there. [pause]

Somewhere below the ridge a bird makes one sound and then decides against making another.

You lean your head back against the wood.

The dome above you is a curve you cannot see the edges of. It could be six feet away. It could be sixty. In the red dark it has no distance at all, and there is something restful in that, in a ceiling that has agreed not to be measured tonight. [softly] Nothing in here needs measuring for a while.

Your hands are on the arms of the chair. They are a little cold. That is fine. That is what the blanket over the back of the chair is for, and you pull it down without getting up, and it settles over your legs smelling faintly of dust and cedar and every other night you have done exactly this.

The sky through the slot has gone from blue to something past blue.

There are three stars now. Then five. You do not count them on purpose. They arrive the way the first one did, without announcement, and the counting happens somewhere behind your attention while the rest of you sits still.

[pause]

The mountain is quiet in a way that is not silence. There is a sound in it — a low continuous nothing, the sound of a great deal of air moving slowly over a great deal of rock, miles of it, none of it in a hurry. It has been doing that all day. It will do it all night. [softly] It does not need you to listen.

But you listen anyway, and it is very easy, because there is nothing in it. No words. No shape. Just the long soft nothing of high air.

You could sleep here. People have. There is a cot in the back room with a wool blanket, and a kettle, and a shelf of paperbacks so swollen with mountain damp that they will not close properly anymore.

Someone left a pair of wool socks on the radiator years ago and nobody has claimed them and nobody has thrown them away, and by now they are simply part of the building. [softly] There is a lot in this building like that. A mug with no handle. A calendar from a year that is over. A jar of pencils, all of them sharpened by hand, none of them by the same hand.

Nothing here is new and nothing here is broken enough to replace.

You are not going back there yet.

The last blue leaves the slot in the dome. [pause] And now the sky is doing the thing it does, the thing you drove nine miles of switchbacks for, which is that it will not stop giving you more.

You look at one small patch of it — a patch the size of your thumbnail held out at arm's length — and there are four stars in it. You keep looking. There are seven. You keep looking, and you stop trying, and there are more than you can hold, and they were all there the entire time. [softly] They were there this afternoon. They were there while you drove.

It is only that the light had to leave before you could have them.

The air through the open slot is cold now and clean and it moves across your face very slowly.

You should log the temperature. There is a book for it, a real paper book with a soft cover, and a pencil on a string. You will. In a while. The night is fourteen hours long at this time of year and there is no part of it that requires hurrying. [pause]

Above you the telescope makes a small sound as the metal contracts in the cooling air. A tick. Then, a long time later, another tick.

You have spent enough nights here to know that the ticking will slow and then stop, the way a stone gives up its heat, the way anything does. Nothing is wrong. It is only the day leaving the metal.

[pause]

The logbook is on the desk under the red lamp, and you reach over and turn it toward you without leaning very far.

It is the fourth logbook, or the fifth. The others are on the shelf behind you in a row, their spines gone soft. This one is half full. You turn back a few pages, not looking for anything, and there is your own handwriting from two weeks ago, and before that somebody else's — smaller, more upright, with the sevens crossed. [softly] You have never met them. You know their handwriting better than you know most people's faces.

Clear, they wrote. Then: Clear. Then: Clear, some high cloud after two.

That is most of what is in the book. Six hundred pages of weather, in a dozen different hands, going back further than the shelf.

You turn forward again to the empty line with tonight's date at the top of it and you leave it empty for now. [pause]

There is a thermos beside the book. You had forgotten about it, which is a small gift, and you pour a little into the cup without sitting up properly, and hold it, and do not drink it yet. The heat comes through the metal into your palms and it is better than drinking would be.

Steam goes up past your face and disappears into the cold.

[softly] You drink some. It is too hot. That is correct too.

Above you, the dome is a curve without an edge and the slot is a stripe of stars, and the whole assembly — dome, telescope, chair, blanket, thermos, book — has been arranged over eighty years by people who wanted, more or less, exactly what you want tonight, which is to sit still in the dark and be shown something.

[pause]

You think about the light coming down the tube.

Not hard. Not in a way that requires anything of you. Only that it is pleasant, sitting in a warm blanket in a cold room, to think that the light arriving in the mirror above you left where it came from a long time ago. Some of it left before the road was built. Some of it left before there was anyone on this mountain to build one.

It has been travelling this entire time. Through nothing. Not slowing. Not tiring. [softly] Not aware of arriving.

And tonight the last little bit of that journey is nine miles of switchbacks and an open slot in a dome, and a person in a wooden chair, with cold hands, under a blanket that smells like cedar.

The thought does not go anywhere after that. It settles. It becomes another quiet thing in the room.

Your eyes have been adjusting this whole time without your permission.

You notice it the way you notice that a room has been getting warm — all at once, retroactively, having missed the process entirely. The stars in the slot are not points anymore. They have colors. There is an orange one low down, and a very cold white one higher up, and between them a scattering of small ones that are only there when you look slightly to the side of them. [pause]

That is a real thing, the looking-slightly-to-the-side. There is a name for it. The center of your eye is built for daylight and the edges are built for this, and so the faintest things in the sky can only be seen by not quite looking at them.

[softly] You have always liked that. That some things arrive only when you stop aiming at them.

The chair holds you. The blanket holds the warm.

Down the mountain, very far away, a set of headlights moves along the valley road — two small yellow points travelling slowly right to left, taking a long time, disappearing behind a shoulder of rock, appearing again smaller. Someone driving home. [pause] They do not know you are up here. You do not need them to.

The lights go behind the last ridge and do not come out.

Now there is nothing manmade in your field of view at all.

You let your shoulders down. You had not known they were up. They come down in two stages, the way they do, and the second one is lower than you expected, and you make a small sound without meaning to.

[softly] It is very quiet up here.

The long nothing of the high air keeps going. The dome ticks once more, further apart now.

You should log the temperature.

In a while.

[pause]

The sky has turned a little. Not enough to see, but the orange star that was low is a bit lower, and the whole slot's worth of sky has slid west by the width of a finger while you were not attending to it. That is what it does all night. It comes up on one side and goes down the other and it has never once been in a hurry about it, not for anyone, not for any reason, in all the time there has been anyone to watch.

You could set your watch by it. People did, for a very long time. [softly] That was the entire job, once — to sit in the cold and watch things cross, and write down when.

There is something restful in being part of a very old and very slow profession.

Your feet are warm under the blanket. Your hands, on the arms of the chair, are not, and you tuck them in, and now everything is warm, and the only cold left is the small clean band of air moving across your face from the slot.

That is the correct arrangement. Warm body. Cold face. Dark room. [pause]

The stars go on arriving.

You are not counting anymore. You stopped some time ago without noticing that you stopped. There is a smear of pale light up the middle of the slot now, so faint that it lives only at the edges of your looking, and it is the flat of the galaxy seen from inside it — the whole disc of it, edge-on, the way you see a plate by looking along its rim.

[softly] All of it moving. None of it appearing to.

The chair is very comfortable. It should not be. It is a hundred years old and made of oak and someone built it for a person shaped differently than you, and still, with the blanket, it is the most comfortable chair you know.

Something crosses the slot.

Not fast. A small steady point of light going from one edge toward the other, not blinking, not flaring — a satellite, catching sun up there that set for you two hours ago. It takes most of a minute to cross. [pause] You watch the whole crossing. There is no reason to and you do it anyway.

It goes out partway. Not gone behind anything — simply into the shadow of the earth, the sunlight running out for it the way it already ran out for you, and the point of light thins and dims and is not there.

[softly] It is still up there. It is only unlit now.

You wait to see whether another one comes. One does, later, going the other way, dimmer. Then nothing for a long while.

The dome could be rotated. There is a motor for it, and a switch, and the sound it makes is enormous in here — a grinding, rolling, iron sound that goes right up through the chair and the floor and your teeth, and takes forty seconds, and always startles the birds off the roof.

You are not going to rotate the dome tonight. [pause] The slot is pointed at a piece of sky you are content with and the sky will bring you new things through it without being asked. That is the arrangement.

The ticking has stopped. The metal has given up the last of the day.

[pause]

Somewhere behind you, in the back room, the kettle is cold on the ring and the paperbacks are swelling slightly in the damp and none of it needs anything.

The mountain keeps making its long low sound.

You will log the temperature. It will say what it says. Cold. Getting colder. The pencil is on its string and the book is on the desk and the red lamp is still on, and it will still be on in an hour, and in four hours, and the numbers will get written down at some point in a hand that gets rounder as the night goes.

That is later.

Right now there is only the slot, and the slow west drift, and the blanket, and the cold clean band of air. [softly] And the light still coming down, the way it has been coming for longer than there have been eyes.

Your eyelids are heavy in the specific way they get in the cold when the rest of you is warm.

You let them close for a moment. Just for a moment. The stars are still there behind them — the after-image of the bright one, orange, fading slowly toward nothing at the center of the dark.

[pause]

When you open them the sky has moved again. A finger's width. Maybe two.

You did not miss anything. There is nothing to miss. It will go on doing this until the blue comes back the other way, hours and hours from now, and then it will do it again tomorrow, and it did it yesterday while you were asleep in town, and it will do it on every night that no one at all is watching.

That is the restful part. [softly] It is not waiting on you.

You settle further into the wood.
